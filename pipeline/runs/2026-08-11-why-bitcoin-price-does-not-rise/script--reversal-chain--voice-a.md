### MOVEMENT 1 — where the money went · 1:10–2:20

> Start with the ETFs, because the ETFs were the whole thesis. Billions of dollars of brand-new
> demand coming in through a pipe that did not exist before. And I want to be fair to this: the
> inflows are real. The money went in. Nobody made that up.
>
> ⟨the obvious reading, stated generously⟩ More demand, same fixed supply, price goes up. That is not
> a stupid model. That is the model basically everyone was running, including me.

**[1:35] ◀ TURN 1**

> But an inflow into an ETF is not the same thing as somebody buying Bitcoin.
>
> When your money lands at a spot Bitcoin ETF, the fund does not sprint out and buy coins that
> afternoon. The actual work is done by authorised participants — the market makers who create and
> redeem ETF shares — and they will routinely create and short those shares *before* they touch any
> underlying Bitcoin. So the purchase is lagged. And it's partly hedged.
>
> ⟨analogy — spent once, drawn from the profile's reference world⟩ The inflow is a pull request, not a
> deploy. It has been opened, it is sitting in the queue, and somebody still has to review it, and it
> might get merged in a form you would not recognise. A repo full of open PRs and a production server
> running last month's code are both true at the same time.

**[2:00] escalation**

> And when the buying does show up, somebody is standing there waiting for it.
>
> ⟨scale conversion⟩ On-chain data suggests long-term holders sold about three point six seven
> million Bitcoin into the hundred-to-a-hundred-and-twenty-six-thousand-dollar range. That is a bigger
> distribution than any previous cycle in Bitcoin's history. The people who held longest sold the
> most, and they sold into exactly the demand that was supposed to take us to the moon.
>
> The ETF wasn't a wave lifting the boat. It was an exit.

### MOVEMENT 2 — the buyers who would never sell · 2:20–3:40

> Which brings us to the other great structural buyer, and my personal favourite financial object of
> the last five years: the treasury companies. Corporations that borrowed money and issued stock for
> the express purpose of holding Bitcoin forever. Strategy, the biggest of them, made "we never sell"
> an actual identity. It was in the bio.
>
> ⟨mechanism⟩ Here's how the machine worked, and it's genuinely clever. If the market values your
> company at more than the Bitcoin sitting on your balance sheet — that ratio is called mNAV — then
> you can issue new shares, spend the money on Bitcoin, and every existing shareholder wakes up owning
> more Bitcoin per share than they did yesterday. It's accretive. It works. And it makes you a buyer
> who genuinely does not care what the price is.
>
> ⟨analogy — the second and last⟩ It's a recursive function whose base case is your own share price.
> Every call buys more Bitcoin, which is supposed to lift the share price, which funds the next call.
> Beautiful while it's running.

**[3:00] ◀ TURN 2**

> But the machine only turns one way.
>
> The second your share price drops below the value of the Bitcoin you're holding — the second mNAV
> goes under one — issuing shares to buy more Bitcoin destroys value instead of creating it. So the
> buying stops. Nobody lost conviction. The arithmetic just inverted.
>
> ⟨scale conversion⟩ Strategy's mNAV went from three point eight nine — the market paying almost four
> dollars for every dollar of Bitcoin the company held — to under one. The stock fell about seventy
> percent between August 2025 and February 2026. They reported a twelve point four billion dollar
> loss for the fourth quarter of 2025.
>
> And in June of this year, Strategy sold thirty two Bitcoin.
>
> ⟨scale conversion — the point is the sentence, not the number⟩ Thirty two coins is about two million
> dollars, from a company holding billions. As a trade it's a rounding error. As a sentence —
> *Strategy sold Bitcoin* — it ended the thing the entire model was built on.

**[3:25] ⟨STEEL-MAN — the script attacks its own argument⟩**

> Now I have to be careful, because the obvious next move is to call these people forced sellers, and
> that's not what the evidence says. Strategy is sitting on more than two and a half years of debt and
> dividend payments in cash. Saylor says they could service six billion in debt with Bitcoin at eight
> thousand dollars. Nobody is getting liquidated.
>
> And on-chain, long-term holders added roughly three hundred and eighty thousand Bitcoin in one month
> this year — almost two percent of the whole supply — *after* the crash, not before.
>
> So this isn't Bitcoin dying. It's a mechanism changing direction.

### MOVEMENT 3 — why Washington didn't matter · 3:40–4:45

> Which leaves politics. And on paper the politics could not have gone better.
>
> March 2025, the President signs an executive order creating a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve. The United
> States government, formally holding Bitcoin as a reserve asset. For a fifteen-year argument about
> whether this thing was even legitimate, that's the end of the argument. You won.

**[4:05] ◀ TURN 3**

> Except sixteen months later the reserve still isn't built. The White House admits the process is
> "being worked out." Federal agencies cannot agree on how much Bitcoin the government actually owns.
> It's a feature announcement with no implementation behind it.
>
> And even where the policy did land — bill passed, regulators swapped, retirement accounts opened —
> the price fell anyway. Because regulatory permission is not demand. Being allowed to buy something
> is not the same as wanting to.

**[4:20] ◀ TURN 4 — the thesis**

> So institutional adoption failed?
>
> No. It succeeded completely. **That's what did this.**
>
> ⟨mechanism — the thesis⟩ When institutions buy an asset, that asset goes into portfolios that are
> governed by risk models. It gets sized. It gets hedged. It gets rebalanced. It stops being a
> conviction and starts being a position — and positions get cut when the environment turns.
>
> So when Treasury yields climbed toward four and a half percent, and thirty-year yields touched
> five, Bitcoin got sold. Not by people who stopped believing. By people rebalancing a risk book.
>
> ⟨scale conversion⟩ Bitcoin now trades with a correlation to the Nasdaq of about zero point seven to
> zero point eight. Most days it's a tech stock with extra steps. And it moves on the ten-year
> Treasury yield, which is the single most boring number in finance.

### REFRAME CLOSE · 4:45–5:00

> Bitcoin spent fifteen years arguing it was an escape from the financial system.
>
> It won that argument. And the prize for winning was getting absorbed into the thing it was escaping
> — priced by the same interest rates, held in the same portfolios, sold in the same risk-off
> afternoons as everything else.
>
> The price isn't broken. The story is. Bitcoin didn't fail to become an institutional asset. It
> succeeded, and institutional assets don't go up forever just because you believe in them.
