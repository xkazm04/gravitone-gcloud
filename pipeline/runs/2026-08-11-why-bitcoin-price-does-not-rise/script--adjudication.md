> Bitcoin peaked at a hundred and twenty six thousand dollars in October 2025. It is now worth

### CANDIDATE 1 — "it's just the cycle" · 0:35–1:15
⟨**the premise-challenging candidate.** Engine D is only honest if the candidate set contains the
possibility that there is nothing to explain. This one goes first, deliberately.⟩

> **State it fairly.** Bitcoin has done this four times. It runs up, it overshoots, it falls fifty to
> eighty percent, it spends a year or two flat, and then it does it again. On this reading nothing has
> changed and there is nothing to explain — you are looking at a normal drawdown and calling it a
> crisis.
>
> **Test it.** It is the strongest of the four, and it has real support: long-term holders added
> roughly three hundred and eighty thousand Bitcoin in a single month this year, *after* the fall.
> That is accumulation, not capitulation. That is what the middle of a cycle looks like.
>
> **Verdict.** Not dismissed — but it explains the *shape* and not the *timing*. Every previous cycle
> fell without a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve, without spot ETFs, without corporate treasuries. The cycle
> theory cannot tell you why the largest demand shock in Bitcoin's history arrived and produced
> nothing. So: partly right. Incomplete.

---

### CANDIDATE 2 — "the ETF demand was never real" · 1:15–2:00

> **State it fairly.** ETF inflows are reported as new demand, but an inflow is not a purchase.
> Authorised participants create and short ETF shares before buying the underlying, so the buying is
> lagged and partly hedged. And what does arrive gets absorbed: long-term holders sold about three
> point six seven million Bitcoin into the hundred-to-a-hundred-and-twenty-six-thousand range — a
> bigger distribution than any previous cycle.
>
> **Test it.** This is well evidenced and it genuinely explains the flat chart alongside the positive
> flow headlines. The ETF was an exit as much as an entrance.
>
> **Verdict.** True, and underrated. But it is a *mechanism*, not a cause. It tells you how the selling
> was delivered. It does not tell you why the sellers wanted out.

---

### CANDIDATE 3 — "the treasury companies broke" · 2:00–2:50

> **State it fairly.** Corporate treasuries were the permanent bid. The mechanism: when your stock
> trades above the Bitcoin on your balance sheet, you issue shares, buy more, and every shareholder
> ends up with more Bitcoin per share. Genuinely accretive — and it makes you a buyer who ignores
> price.
>
> Then it inverts. Strategy's ratio went from three point eight nine to below one. The stock fell
> about seventy percent. The company posted a twelve point four billion dollar quarterly loss. And in
> June it sold thirty two Bitcoin — trivial money, but the end of "we never sell."
>
> **Test it.** Here is where I have to be honest against my own case: they are not forced sellers.
> Strategy holds over two and a half years of debt and dividend coverage in cash. Nobody is
> liquidating. The thirty two coins were symbolic, not structural.
>
> **Verdict.** Real, and it removed a large price-insensitive buyer from the market. But a missing
> buyer is not the same as a reason to sell.

---

### CANDIDATE 4 — "it's macro" · 2:50–4:00

> **State it fairly.** Ten-year Treasury yields climbed toward four and a half percent; thirty-year
> yields touched five. The dollar strengthened. Tariffs and geopolitics did what they do. Risk assets
> sold off, and Bitcoin — which yields nothing — sold off with them.
>
> **Test it.** Bitcoin's correlation with the Nasdaq now sits around zero point seven to zero point
> eight. It moves with equities, against real yields, and with global liquidity. Every piece of that
> checks out.
>
> **Verdict — and the reason this is the answer.** On its own, "it's macro" sounds like an excuse.
> Every asset has macro. The question was why *this* asset, with the best fundamental news of its
> life, behaved like an ordinary one.
>
> ⟨**the reframe — the verdict is the thesis**⟩ And that is the answer. It behaved like an ordinary
> risk asset **because it became one.** Institutional adoption was not a demand event, it was a
> *category change*. When institutions buy something, it enters portfolios governed by risk models —
> sized, hedged, rebalanced, and cut when the environment turns. The adoption that was supposed to
> make Bitcoin valuable is precisely what made it normal.
>
> Candidates one, two and three are all describing the same thing from different angles. The cycle,
> the ETF plumbing, the treasury flywheel — those are the *transmission*. The cause is that Bitcoin
> won its argument.
