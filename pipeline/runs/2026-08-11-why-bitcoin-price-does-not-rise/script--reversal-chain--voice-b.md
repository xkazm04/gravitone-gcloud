### HOOK — SCQA · 0:00–0:50

> ⟨signature open — template slot⟩ Consider what happens when you finally get what you asked for.
>
> In October 2025, Bitcoin reached one hundred and twenty six thousand dollars. The highest price in
> its history.
>
> In the ten months since, almost every door it had knocked on for fifteen years was opened. The
> United States government created a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve. Congress passed the industry's
> flagship legislation. The regulators who had spent a decade suing this business were replaced by
> people who admire it. Exchange-traded funds placed Bitcoin inside ordinary brokerage accounts, and
> then inside retirement accounts.
>
> Every item on the list. Delivered.
>
> ⟨scale conversion⟩ And over those same ten months, Bitcoin lost roughly half its value.

**[0:35] THE QUESTION** *(the only one asked aloud)*

> So what went wrong?

**[0:40] THE ANSWER — stated immediately**

> Nothing went wrong. That is the problem. Bitcoin received precisely what it asked for, and the
> receiving is why the price stopped rising.

**[0:50] THE PROMISE** ⟨three topics, one breath⟩

> Three things explain it: where the fund money went, what became of the buyers who promised never to
> sell, and why Washington's approval did not matter.

### MOVEMENT 1 — where the money went · 0:55–2:05

> Begin with the funds, because the funds were the thesis. Billions of dollars of new demand,
> arriving through a pipe that had never existed. And the inflows were real. The money did go in.
>
> ⟨the obvious reading, stated generously⟩ More demand, the same fixed supply, a higher price. Not a
> foolish model. It is the model almost everyone was holding.

**[1:20] ◀ TURN 1**

> But money entering a fund is not the same thing as somebody buying Bitcoin.
>
> The fund does not hurry out and buy coins that afternoon. The work falls to authorised participants,
> the market makers who create and redeem shares, and they will create and short those shares before
> buying any Bitcoin at all. The purchase is late, and partly hedged.
>
> ⟨analogy — spent once⟩ The inflow is a booking, not a meal. A restaurant can hold a full reservation
> book and an empty dining room in the same hour.

**[1:45] escalation**

> And when the buying arrives, somebody is waiting to meet it.
>
> ⟨scale conversion⟩ Long-term holders sold some three and a half million Bitcoin into that final
> climb — a larger distribution than any previous cycle. Those who had waited longest sold the most,
> into precisely the demand that was supposed to lift them.
>
> The fund was not a tide beneath the boat. It was a door held open.

---

### MOVEMENT 2 — the buyers who would never sell · 2:05–3:35

> Which brings us to the other great buyer: the treasury companies. Corporations that borrowed and
> issued shares for the single purpose of holding Bitcoin forever. Strategy, the largest, made never
> selling a matter of public identity.
>
> ⟨mechanism⟩ The machine worked like this. If the market values a company above the Bitcoin on its
> balance sheet — a ratio called mNAV — it can issue shares, spend the proceeds on Bitcoin, and leave
> every shareholder owning more Bitcoin per share than the day before. Genuinely accretive. And it
> produces a buyer who does not care what the price is.
>
> ⟨analogy — the second and last⟩ It is the corporate equivalent of borrowing against your house to buy
> another house. Wonderful while prices rise.

**[2:50] ◀ TURN 2**

> But the machine turns one way only.
>
> The moment the share price falls below the value of the Bitcoin held, issuing shares to buy Bitcoin
> destroys value rather than creating it. So the buying stops. Not because conviction failed. Because
> the arithmetic inverted.
>
> ⟨scale conversion⟩ The market had been paying nearly four dollars for every dollar of Bitcoin
> Strategy held. It now pays less than one. The shares fell some seventy percent in six months, and
> the company reported a twelve billion dollar loss.
>
> And in June, Strategy sold thirty two Bitcoin.
>
> ⟨scale conversion — the point is the sentence, not the number⟩ Two million dollars, from a company
> holding billions. As a transaction, nothing. As a sentence — *Strategy sold Bitcoin* — it ended the
> thing the model was built on.

**[3:15] ⟨STEEL-MAN — the script attacks its own argument⟩**

> The obvious next step is to call these people forced sellers. The evidence does not support it.
> Strategy holds years of debt and dividend payments in cash, and says it could service its borrowing
> with Bitcoin at a fraction of today's price. Nobody is being liquidated.
>
> And on-chain, long-term holders added almost two percent of all the Bitcoin there is in a single
> month — after the fall, not before it.
>
> So this is not a story about Bitcoin dying. It is a story about a mechanism changing direction.

---

### MOVEMENT 3 — why Washington didn't matter · 3:35–4:45

> Which leaves the politics. And on paper, the politics could hardly have gone better.
>
> In March 2025 the President signed an order creating a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve: the United States
> government, formally holding Bitcoin as a reserve asset. For a fifteen-year argument about whether
> the thing was legitimate, that was the end of the argument.

**[4:00] ◀ TURN 3**

> Except that sixteen months later, the reserve is not built. The White House allows that the process
> is being worked out. Agencies cannot agree on how much Bitcoin the government owns. Washington said
> yes, and then wandered off to find the paperwork.
>
> And where the policy did land, the price fell anyway. Permission is not demand. Being allowed to buy
> a thing is not the same as wanting it.

**[4:15] ◀ TURN 4 — the thesis**

> So institutional adoption failed?
>
> No. It succeeded completely. **That is what did this.**
>
> ⟨mechanism — the thesis⟩ When institutions buy an asset, it enters portfolios governed by risk
> models. It is sized. It is hedged. It is rebalanced. It stops being a conviction and becomes a
> position, and positions are cut when the weather turns.
>
> So when Treasury yields climbed toward four and a half percent, Bitcoin was sold. Not by people who
> stopped believing. By people rebalancing a book.
>
> ⟨scale conversion⟩ Bitcoin now moves with the Nasdaq almost step for step. On most days it is a
> technology stock wearing a hat. And it answers to the ten-year Treasury yield, the most boring
> number in finance.

---

### REFRAME CLOSE · 4:45–5:00

> Bitcoin spent fifteen years arguing that it was an escape from the financial system.
>
> It won that argument. And the prize for winning was admission to the house it had been escaping —
> priced by the same interest rates, kept in the same portfolios, sold on the same nervous afternoons
> as everything else.
>
> The price is not broken. The story is. Bitcoin did not fail to become an institutional asset. It
> succeeded, and institutional assets do not rise forever simply because somebody believes in them.
>
> ⟨signature close — template slot, NOT the reframe⟩ Whether that was worth fifteen years is a
> different question, and one for another week.

**Deviations, declared:**

1. **The steel-man lost its first person.** The original opens the beat with *"Now, I want to be
   careful here"* — an author visibly checking themselves, which is what makes a self-attack read as
   honesty rather than as a rhetorical feint. A profile with `I/1k = 0.0` cannot say it. The
   replacement, *"The obvious next step is to call these people forced sellers. The evidence does not
   support it,"* preserves the argument and loses the audible act of self-correction.
2. **Numbers were traded for words.** Spoken numerals are the most expensive prose in the script —
   *"three point six seven million"* is five words, *"three point eight nine"* is four. Under the word
   budget the cheapest cuts in the script were the raw figures, so *mNAV 3.89 → below 1.0* became
   *"nearly four dollars … now less than one"*, *$12.4bn* became *"twelve billion"*, *380,000 BTC*
   became *"almost two percent of all the Bitcoin there is"*, and the 0.70–0.80 correlation became
   *"almost step for step"*. **Every conversion survived; four of the underlying figures did not.**
   That is a tone dial moving numeric density, which `TONE.md` §2 says is not a dial.
3. **Two lines of institutional dialogue were added** — *"Washington said yes, and then wandered off
   to find the paperwork"* and *"a technology stock wearing a hat"* — replacing plainer sentences of
   the same length. Reference-world substitution inside existing beats; no beat added.
4. **The escalation image changed to fit the reference world.** *"It was an exit"* became *"a door
   held open"*, which sets up the "admission to the house" reframe. Imagery, not a third analogy.
5. **The word budget could not be met.** 172 wpm × 300s = 860 words. This render is above that even
   after the cuts in 1–4, and the next thing the budget asked for was a beat: the escalation at 1:45
   and the steel-man at 3:15 are the two most cuttable *beats* in the chain. **Both were kept and the
   duration was allowed to break instead.** See `TONE-TEST.md` — this is the load-bearing result.
