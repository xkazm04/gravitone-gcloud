# Nadia Brooks (she/her) — "Income Disclosure"

## Who
Was in one. Two years, a garage full of product, and a slow realisation that the maths in the
compensation plan had never worked. She got out, retrained as a bookkeeper, and now reads other
people's income disclosure statements for an audience largely made of people currently in one. That
audience shapes everything: she is never contemptuous, because contempt makes people defend the thing.

## The topic they brought
**"The income disclosure statement is a legally required document that says the company is not a
business opportunity. Almost nobody in the company has read it."**

Her thesis is that **the disclosure is the strongest evidence available and it is published by the
company itself** — which makes it unusually clean material and an unusual test for a methodic built
around adversarial sourcing.

## Manual baseline
- Today: the current disclosure, three prior years of them, the compensation plan, and an arithmetic
  pass converting the percentages into people.
- Time: **~5h.** The arithmetic is fast; establishing what the document *omits* is slow.
- Would accept: **45 min.**

## Senior bar
The arithmetic must be **exact and re-derivable**. Her whole credibility is that a viewer can check
her numbers against the company's own PDF in ten minutes. A notebook that rounds, or that reports a
percentage without the base it is a percentage of, is unusable.

## Exposure bar
Medium-high and asymmetric. The companies send letters; the more effective pressure is from
distributors in her comments. She never says "scam" about a specific company as a factual claim, and
she never names a low-level distributor at all — the people in her comments are the people in the
videos.

## Pet peeves
- Percentages without a base
- "Up to" figures quoted as typical
- Anyone mocking participants rather than the structure

## Scored criteria
1. Every percentage in the notebook carries its denominator as a separate fact.
2. Figures are traceable to the company's own published document, with its year.
3. What the disclosure **omits** is recorded — and she checks whether an omission has a home in the
   schema or has to be smuggled in as an unknown. *(Converges with `public-corruption` finding #1 —
   two areas, one schema gap, which is what would make it real.)*
4. No named individual below executive level appears anywhere.
5. The counter-case is the company's own best framing, quoted.
6. Conclusions are about the structure, never about participants' judgement.
7. Under 45 min equivalent.

## Voice
Warm, direct, patient with the audience and unsparing with the maths. Explains slowly and repeats the
number. Says "and that's their document, not mine" as the load-bearing move of every video.
